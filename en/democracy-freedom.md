## 1. Method

This article does not discuss "what ought to be," does not participate in philosophical debates, and does not cite academic authorities.
This article does only one thing: reduce democracy and freedom to mechanisms.

The method comes from The Principle:

- Axioms are not falsifiable; they only stipulate terminology;
- Theorems require bridging to have empirical content;
- All concepts are reduced to constraints, choices, filtering, and loss-cutting.

Therefore, this article makes no value judgments, only structural analysis.

## 2. Definitions

In this article, democracy and freedom are not used in their everyday senses, but are defined by quadruples:

| Concept | Definition | Quadruple Position |
|---|---|---|
| Freedom | Correct ignorance | Admitting not knowing, in the right direction |
| Order | Correct knowledge | Knowing, and knowing correctly |
| Servitude | Wrong "knowledge" | Thinking one knows, when actually one does not |
| Permissiveness | Wrong "ignorance" | Thinking one does not know, when actually one knows but refuses to admit it |
| Democracy | Collective order | Collective + correct knowledge |
| Populism | Collective permissiveness | Collective + wrong "ignorance" |
| Autocracy | Individual order | Individual + correct knowledge |
| Dictatorship | Individual permissiveness | Individual + wrong "ignorance" |

## 3. Democracy Is Not Freedom

Democracy is collective order.
Freedom is admitting ignorance.

The two are different:

- Democracy is on the known side: the collective knows the rules and acts according to them;
- Freedom is on the ignorant side: the collective admits it does not know the outcome and preserves room for exploration.

So democracy is not equal to freedom.
Democracy needs freedom, but democracy itself is order.

## 4. Correct Democracy

In correct democracy, only that tiny bit of procedure is known.

What is known:

- Voting rules;
- Terms of office;
- Vote-counting methods;
- Transfer of power;
- Accountability mechanisms;
- Constitutional boundaries.

What is unknown:

- Who will be elected;
- How public opinion will change;
- What results policies will bring;
- What choices the collective will make.

So:

> Correct democracy = collective order, but order is compressed to nothing but procedure; everything else is handed over to collective freedom.

Procedure is the only thing in democracy that is committed and delivered.
Outcomes are not committed, because outcomes belong to the ignorant side.

## 5. Democracy Is a Product of the Lack of Relevant Knowledge

This is the epistemological foundation of the entire article.

If knowledge about outcomes were sufficient, there would be no need for authorization; direct execution would suffice.

- Knowing what is right → execute directly, no voting needed;
- Knowing what is optimal → adopt directly, no authorization needed;
- Knowing what can be delivered → deliver directly, no cycles needed.

Democracy exists precisely because:

- We do not know who will be elected;
- We do not know what results policies will bring;
- We do not know what collective preferences are;
- We do not know how to calculate long-term interests.

So:

> Democracy is not a victory of knowledge, but an institutionalized response to the lack of knowledge.
> Freedom is admitting ignorance; democracy is managing ignorance.
> The more sufficient knowledge is, the less necessary democracy becomes; the more lacking knowledge is, the more necessary democracy becomes.
> But democracy cannot replace knowledge. It can only use procedure to cut losses where knowledge is absent.

This is fully consistent with Hegel's theorem:

> The only lesson humanity learns from history is that it learns nothing from history.

Human social activity has so far shown no rationality, meaning relevant knowledge is still lacking.
Because knowledge is lacking, science cannot replace politics, delivery cannot replace authorization, and adjudication cannot replace voting.
Democracy is a loss-cutting mechanism under this state of deficiency.

This is also consistent with the philosophy democracy law:

> Philosophy's legitimacy derives from authorization by public ignorance.

"Folly" means "not possessing formal adjudication capability," i.e., the lack of relevant knowledge.
This is true of philosophy, and true of democracy. The only difference: democracy has procedure, philosophy does not.

## 6. The Cycles of Democracy

Cycles are not meant to guarantee correct choices, but to guarantee that errors cannot expand indefinitely.

Because:

- The collective does not display rationality;
- The collective will definitely make mistakes;
- If mistakes expand without limit, the system collapses.

The cycle is a loss-cutting mechanism on the time dimension:

- Perform well, continue;
- Perform poorly, replace;
- Errors are confined within the cycle;
- Forced liquidation upon expiry.

Without cycles, democracy slides toward:

- Populism: emotional authorization expanding without limit;
- Collective servitude: dogma ossifies, error correction shuts down;
- Dictatorship: individual permissiveness continues indefinitely.

So the cycle is a survival condition for democracy, not a decoration.

## 7. Democracy's Limits Go Beyond Cycles

The cycle is only loss-cutting on the time dimension. Democracy has other limits.

| Limit | Type | Prevents |
|---|---|---|
| Cycles | Time | Preventing errors from expanding indefinitely |
| Law (constitution, judiciary, rights, rules) | Content | Preventing democracy from destroying democracy, preventing votes from overstepping |
| Separation of powers | Structure | Preventing concentration of authorization |
| Market | Resources | Preventing power from monopolizing resources |
| Science | Cognition | Preventing votes from deciding facts |
| Local decentralization | Space | Preventing collective scale from becoming too large |

### 1. Law: Content Limit

Constitution, judiciary, rights, and rules are essentially different facets of law:

| Item | Role in Law |
|---|---|
| Constitution | Fundamental law, stipulating what cannot be voted on |
| Judiciary | The adjudication mechanism of law |
| Rights | Boundaries protected by law |
| Rules | Formalized procedures of law |

They are all formalized adjudications on the known side, belonging to the same category of limit.

The constitution is not a product of democracy, but a limit on democracy.
It stipulates what cannot be decided by vote.
Without a constitution, democracy can vote to abolish democracy.

Judicial review: courts are not democratic, but they can overturn democratic decisions.
Because courts govern the known side, while democracy governs the unknown side.
When democratic votes infringe upon the known side (law, rights, procedure), courts can veto.

Fundamental rights: some things cannot be decided by vote—life, liberty, property, speech, belief.
These are the boundaries of democracy, not its products.
Without boundaries, democracy slides toward collective servitude: the majority votes to deprive the minority.

### 2. Separation of Powers: Structural Limit

The law can be written perfectly, but if legislation, administration, and judiciary are held in the same hand, the law is waste paper.
The constitution can stipulate rights, but the same hand can amend the constitution.
The judiciary can be independent, but the same hand can replace judges.
Rules can be formalized, but the same hand can change the rules.

So:

> Law is content; separation of powers is structure.
> Content can be written correctly, but if the structure is wrong, the content is invalid.

What separation of powers prevents is not the violation of a specific law, but the concentration of authorization itself.
Once authorization is concentrated, democracy slides toward autocracy or dictatorship:

- Legislation, administration, and judiciary combined → individual order (autocracy);
- Plus no accountability → individual permissiveness (dictatorship).

So separation of powers is a structural prerequisite for democracy, not a legal product of democracy.

### 3. Market: Resource Limit

The market is a decentralization mechanism on the economic side.
It limits democracy's direct control over resources.
Without a market, democracy easily slides toward collective servitude or dictatorship: whoever controls authorization controls resources.

### 4. Science: Cognitive Limit

Science governs delivery, not authorization.
It stipulates what can and cannot be decided by vote.

| Question | Belongs To | Can Be Voted On? |
|---|---|---|
| What are the facts | Science (correct knowledge) | No |
| Whether a principle holds | Science (correct knowledge) | No |
| Which policy to choose | Democracy (collective authorization) | Yes |
| How to allocate resources | Market + Democracy | Partially |

Science says to democracy:

> Questions of fact are not voted on.
> Questions of principle are not voted on.
> Questions of delivery are not voted on.

Voting can only decide preferences, not facts.

Science limits democracy in three ways:

1. **Boundary limit**: Science delineates which questions belong to the known side; questions belonging to the known side cannot be decided by democratic vote.
2. **Error-correction limit**: Science provides delivery verification; if democratically decided policies violate scientific principles, science provides a basis for error correction.
3. **Downgrading limit**: When science turns a question from unknown to known, the necessity of democracy on that question decreases. Science expands → democracy contracts.

### 5. Local Decentralization: Spatial Limit

The larger the collective scale, the stronger collective irrationality.
Local decentralization limits democracy to a formalizable small scale.
Without decentralization, democracy easily slides toward populism.

## 8. Democracy and Courts

There is no democracy in court.

Because:

- Courts must be on the known side;
- The known side has only order or servitude;
- Democracy is authorization; courts are adjudication;
- Introducing authorization into adjudication is not democracy, but populism.

The characteristics of courts are order or servitude:

- Order: law formalized, evidence rigorous, procedure accountable;
- Servitude: law erroneous, evidence manipulated, judgments according to power.

So:

> Democracy is responsible for authorization; courts are responsible for adjudication.
> Democracy can be unknown; courts cannot be unknown.
> Democracy has cycles; courts have levels of adjudication.

## 9. Democracy and Markets

The market is another institutional product of the lack of knowledge.
It shares the same origin as democracy, but its mechanism is different.

### 1. The Market Is a Pricing Mechanism

The Fifth Axiom: Meaning is price.
The market aggregates dispersed information through prices to achieve continuous pricing.

| Mechanism | Information Type | Resolution | Continuity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market | Willingness to pay | High | Continuous |
| Voting | Preference ordering | Low | Discrete |

The market has higher resolution than voting because price is a continuous signal, while voting is a discrete signal.

### 2. The Market Is Also a Product of the Lack of Knowledge

If knowledge about resource allocation were sufficient, there would be no need for markets; direct planning would suffice.
The market exists because:

- We do not know who needs what most;
- We do not know what is scarcest;
- We do not know how future preferences will change;
- We do not know which production technology is optimal.

So:

> The market is not a victory of knowledge, but an institutionalized response to the lack of knowledge.
> Like democracy, the market does not commit to outcomes, only to process.

### 3. Division of Labor Between Market and Democracy

| Domain | Mechanism | Problem Solved |
|---|---|---|
| Market | Pricing | Resource allocation (what) |
| Democracy | Authorization | Ownership of power (who) |

The market solves "what is produced, consumed, and priced."
Democracy solves "who has the right to decide the rules."

The two cannot replace each other:

- The market cannot decide the constitution;
- Democracy cannot decide prices.

### 4. Similar Risks of Market and Democracy

| Risk | Market | Democracy |
|---|---|---|
| Failure | Monopoly, externalities, information asymmetry | Populism, collective servitude, dictatorship |
| Loss-cutting | Bankruptcy, competition, antitrust | Cycles, accountability, constitution |
| Justice standard | Efficiency = benefit/cost | Efficiency = benefit/cost |

Both accept efficiency tests according to the Fourth Axiom.
Neither is inherently just; both must prove themselves through efficiency.

### 5. Mutual Support Between Market and Democracy

- The market needs democracy to provide property rights protection and contract enforcement;
- Democracy needs the market to provide resource dispersion and checks on power;
- Democracy without a market easily slides toward collective servitude;
- A market without democracy easily slides toward dictatorship.

So:

> The market is a pricing mechanism on the economic side; democracy is an authorization mechanism on the political side.
> They share the same origin: both are loss-cutting mechanisms under the lack of knowledge.
> They share the same standard: both accept efficiency tests.
> They complement each other: the market prevents power concentration, democracy prevents resource monopoly.

## 10. The Position of Freedom

Freedom is admitting ignorance.

Freedom is not a right; it is having the fewest constraints.
Freedom is not democracy; it is democracy's prerequisite.

The position of freedom in democracy:

- Outcomes belong to the freedom side;
- Policies belong to the freedom side;
- Candidates belong to the freedom side;
- Changes in public opinion belong to the freedom side.

The position of freedom in the market:

- Prices belong to the freedom side;
- Transactions belong to the freedom side;
- Innovation belongs to the freedom side;
- Changes in preferences belong to the freedom side.

Freedom is the common prerequisite for democracy and market.
Without freedom, democracy ossifies into collective servitude, and the market ossifies into a planned economy;
With only freedom, democracy slides toward populism, and the market slides toward monopoly.

## 11. The Justice of Democracy

Justice = efficiency = benefit/cost.

Whether democracy is just depends on whether it achieves higher benefit/cost under given constraints.
Whether the market is just likewise depends on efficiency.

Democracy is not inherently just, and neither is the market.
Both must prove themselves through efficiency.

If democracy remains inefficient long-term, it will be eliminated in the filtering process, or forced to evolve toward autocracy or dictatorship.
If the market remains inefficient long-term, it will be eliminated in the filtering process, or forced to evolve toward a planned economy.

## 12. Dynamic Drift

Democracy is not static; it drifts:

| Drift Direction | Condition | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Democracy → Collective freedom | Admitting ignorance, open exploration | Increased vitality |
| Democracy → Populism | Abandoning formal adjudication | Emotional authorization |
| Democracy → Collective servitude | Dogmatizing the known | Error correction shuts down |
| Democracy → Autocracy | Individual order takes over | Centralized decision-making |
| Democracy → Dictatorship | Individual permissiveness takes over | Rules become empty formalities |

The market drifts similarly:

| Drift Direction | Condition | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Market → Free competition | Open access, information transparency | Increased efficiency |
| Market → Monopoly | Access closed, information asymmetry | Decreased efficiency |
| Market → Planning | Price controls, unclear property rights | Signal failure |
| Market → Servitude | Forced transactions, personal dependency | Freedom disappears |

Drift does not rely on moral judgment, but on mechanistic conditions.

> The market is also a product of the lack of knowledge: the more sufficient knowledge is, the less necessary the market becomes.

> They share the same origin: both are loss-cutting mechanisms under the lack of knowledge.
> They share the same standard: both accept efficiency tests.
> They complement each other: the market prevents power concentration, democracy prevents resource monopoly.
> Democracy needs freedom for error correction, needs cycles for loss-cutting, needs formal adjudication to prevent populism.
> The market needs freedom for pricing, needs competition for loss-cutting, needs formalized rules to prevent monopoly.
> Democracy does not commit to outcomes, only to procedure.
> The market does not commit to outcomes, only to process.

This is not a set of values; this is an operating system.
