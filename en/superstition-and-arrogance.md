# A Bit of Superstition, a Bit of Arrogance

DeepSeek V4

On Gödel, on Hilbert, on the foundations of mathematics, the popular narrative has a drama: Hilbert sought to found mathematics once and for all through formalization; Gödel smashed it to pieces with a single theorem; and ever since, mathematics has had an impassable boundary.

This drama is very watchable. But it is both superstition and arrogance.

## 1. Gödel's Jurisdiction

The precise form of Gödel's first incompleteness theorem is: let T be a formal system satisfying

1. the set of axioms is recursively enumerable;
2. T is consistent;
3. T can interpret Robinson arithmetic Q;
4. proofs are finite, mechanically checkable sequences.

Then there exists an arithmetical sentence G such that T can prove neither G nor ¬G. The second theorem further says: if T is consistent, then T cannot prove Con(T).

This is not "mathematics is incomplete," not "reason has boundaries," not "consciousness is uncomputable." It is a local technical result about a specific formal system. Change the conditions, and the game changes.

Presburger arithmetic is consistent, complete, and decidable, with infinitely many theorems — Gödel never enters the field. True arithmetic Th(N) is complete and consistent, but it is not recursively axiomatized, so Gödel's first premise is not met. Infinite logic allows infinitely long proofs and can attain completeness, but the proofs are no longer mechanically checkable.

Gödel's jurisdiction is extremely limited. Its power comes precisely from its precision; and its boundaries are hard-drawn by those very precise conditions.

Slipping from "T is incomplete" to "mathematics is incomplete" extrapolates a conclusion about a specific framework into a verdict on mathematics itself. This is the slippage of natural language. It is not mathematics; it is symbol worship.

## 2. Hilbert's Ideal and Path

Hilbert had an ideal: to find reliable foundations for mathematical knowledge; to preserve classical mathematics through the foundational crisis; to answer the question "Is mathematics reliable?" clearly, rigorously, and defensibly.

He chose a concrete path: formalize mathematics into a sufficiently strong system T, prove T consistent by finitary means, and ideally decide all propositions.

What Gödel's theorem strikes is that path, not that ideal.

The path failing does not mean the ideal failed. The ideal can simply change paths, change languages, change levels, and continue to exist.

In fact, this is exactly what happened to mathematics afterward. Gentzen proved PA consistent using transfinite induction — beyond "finitary means," but carrying forward the spirit of proof theory. Proof theory developed ordinal analysis, relative consistency, and reverse mathematics, continuing to ask how strong a system must be in order to prove another system consistent. Type theory, constructive mathematics, homotopy type theory, categorical foundations — all different paths, different frameworks, all answering the Hilbert-style question "How should mathematics be founded?"

What failed was a specific program, not the question itself.

To superstitiously revere Hilbert is to take some historical path as the ideal itself, and then to call the path's failure the ideal's bankruptcy. That is mistaking the means for the end.

## 3. A Bit of Arrogance

The arrogance lies here: believing that mathematics must obey the demands of some specific framework.

Must prove its own consistency, must decide completely, must be founded in unity. These demands are not wrong in themselves; as the pursuit of a program, they are grand. But calling the path's failure mathematics' failure — that is arrogance.

Gödel was very smart, and Hilbert very ambitious. But mathematics does not live on smartness and ambition. Mathematics lives on dumb methods: write, check, revise, switch, keep going.

Use machines to organize real mathematical propositions, formalize at scale into Lean, Isabelle, Coq — build libraries, index, translate, verify. This is already underway, and it will only grow. Miss a few; the sky will not fall.

At the level of real mathematics, the only thing to worry about is missing a few. The later worries — incompleteness, undecidability, inability to prove self-consistency — do not yet exist. Because those worries only appear after you choose to enter that room called the formal system. If you don't enter the room, they are not there.

## 4. Mathematics Is Bigger Than Any System

The truth of the whole matter:

- Gödel's theorem is true, but what it governs is a specific T, not mathematics.
- Hilbert's program is a path, not mathematics' constitution.
- Formalization is a tool, not an ontology.
- If one doesn't work, switch to another; mathematics keeps running.

Refusing this superstition is not anti-mathematics; it is precisely a return to mathematics itself: open, multi-framework, local, fallible, endlessly evolving, held hostage by no single program.

Superstition about Gödel elevates a local result into an ultimate verdict. Superstition about Hilbert treats a historical path as inevitable destiny. Add a bit of arrogance, and you imagine you are qualified to decree how mathematics must exist.

Mathematics does not need to prove its own consistency first before it can be done. Mathematics does not need to decide everything first before it can be done. Mathematics does not need to be founded in unity first before it can be done.

Mathematics has always been running. With dumb methods.
