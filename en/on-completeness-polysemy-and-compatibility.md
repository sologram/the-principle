# On Completeness, Polysemy, and Compatibility

## — Guided by the Scale-Free, Self-Referential Principle of Things

---

## Introduction

This paper examines the uses of the concept of "completeness" across different domains: everyday completeness, logical completeness, syntactic completeness, model-theoretic completeness, and ontological completeness. These uses are related but not identical. Conflating them produces polysemy; severing them entirely loses the genuine compatibility that holds among them.

This paper takes **The Principle** as its guide. The Principle is not a theorem of mathematical logic, and it is not a corollary of Gödel's theorems. It is the scale-free principle of self-reference, and it is the condition that makes every discourse of completeness, every judgment of truth, and every game of falsification possible.

The Principle states explicitly:

1. Every thing has its principle; a thing conforms to its principle, and is therefore correct; correctness is completeness.
2. The Principle applies to itself, and is therefore self-referential.
3. The Principle is at no particular level, and operates in the same way at every level, and is therefore scale-free.
4. Self-reference comes in two kinds: correct and erroneous. Erroneous self-reference commonly takes the form of mutable self-reference.
5. The Principle is correct self-reference. Formal systems are scaled; when they attempt full self-reference, they necessarily slide into mutable self-reference.
6. The Principle explicitly rejects falsificationism. Falsificationism is a within-scale regional rule, and does not apply to the scale-free self-referential principle.
7. Mathematical-logical truths depend on The Principle. This dependence is not an empirical hypothesis and cannot be falsified; it is a structural relation of scale-free self-reference.

The goal of this paper is to put The Principle back at the center of the argument as a scale-free self-referential principle, while preserving the triple-formal framework and the exact statement of Gödel's theorems.

The chain of argument runs as follows:

1. The Principle as a scale-free self-referential principle;
2. The distinction between correct self-reference and mutable self-reference;
3. The rejection of falsificationism;
4. The Principle projects into different domains as concrete decision criteria \(C_S\);
5. Completeness is defined using the triple \((S,R,C_S)\);
6. The five kinds of completeness are examined uniformly within that framework;
7. Gödel's theorems are interpreted as the proof that formal systems slide into mutable self-reference;
8. Polysemy is distinguished from structural compatibility;
9. The problems of self-reference, circularity, and infinite regress are addressed.

## 1. The Principle as a Scale-Free Self-Referential Principle

### 1.1 Basic Formulation

The Principle can be formulated as:

> Every thing has its principle. A thing conforms to its principle, and is therefore correct; correctness is completeness.

Here "thing" is taken in the broadest sense: it covers everyday tools, institutions, and works, as well as formal systems, theories, models, and indeed any object that can be judged as to whether it conforms to its own specification.

From this we obtain the core proposition of this paper:

> **Correctness is completeness.**

This is not a theorem of mathematical logic; it is an ontological principle. It fixes the ultimate meaning of "completeness": completeness is not an arbitrary convention, nor something purely formal, but the conformity relation between a thing and its principle.

### 1.2 Self-Reference

The Principle applies to itself.

"Every thing has its principle" — The Principle is itself a thing, so it too has its principle. That principle is not some higher principle, but itself.

In formal language:

- An ordinary principle: \( P(x) \) holds for an object \( x \);
- A self-referential principle: \( P(P) \) also holds.

This is not a vicious circle, but self-inclusion. Similar cases:

- The identity law \( A \to A \) of logic applies to itself;
- Language can talk about language;
- Consciousness can be conscious of itself.

### 1.3 Scale-Freeness

The Principle occupies no particular scale.

It applies both to micro-objects and to macro-objects; both to everyday things and to formal systems; both at the object level and at the meta-level.

This means:

- There is no absolute distinction between a "foundation level" and an "upper level";
- There is no absolute stratification between "first principles" and "derived principles";
- There is no absolute stratification between "metalanguage" and "object language".

The Principle operates in the same way at every level. It is not the first brick at the bottom of the tower, but the structural rule running through the whole tower.

### 1.4 Correct Self-Reference and Mutable Self-Reference

The Principle states explicitly: self-reference comes in two kinds, correct and erroneous. Erroneous self-reference commonly takes the form of mutable self-reference.

**Correct self-reference**:

- The object referred to remains the same throughout the process;
- The act of referring does not change the object;
- It is scale-free;
- It produces no paradox and no infinite regress.

Typical manifestations:

- The identity law \( A \to A \);
- Fractal self-similarity;
- Spinoza's "cause of itself";
- Hegel's "true infinity";
- The Principle itself.

**Mutable self-reference**:

- The object referred to changes in the course of the process;
- The act of referring changes the object;
- It is scaled;
- It produces paradox or infinite regress.

Typical manifestations:

- The liar paradox: "this sentence is false";
- Russell's paradox: \( R = \{x \mid x \notin x\} \);
- The Gödel sentence: \( G \leftrightarrow \neg \mathrm{Prov}(G) \);
- The consistency sentence;
- The truth predicate paradox.

The core of "mutable" is this: self-reference is not self-identity but self-difference. The act of referring changes what is referred to, and self-reference thereby becomes other-reference.

The Principle is correct self-reference. A formal system is scaled; when it attempts full self-reference, it necessarily slides into mutable self-reference.

### 1.5 The Rejection of Falsificationism

The Principle explicitly rejects falsificationism.

Falsificationism is an epistemological rule within scale:

- It presupposes the dichotomy of observation and theory;
- It presupposes a stratification between the empirical level and the theoretical level;
- It presupposes the one-way relation of "testing a theory by experience".

The Principle is a scale-free self-referential principle. It occupies no particular level, and operates in the same way at every level. Therefore, to subject The Principle to falsificationist demands is a category error.

The Principle does not take part in the game of empirical science. Its defense does not come from empirical testing, but from transcendental argument, coherence, explanatory power, and unavoidability.

Unfalsifiability is not a defect of The Principle, but a categorical feature of it as a scale-free self-referential principle.

An attempt to falsify The Principle is itself already a slide into mutable self-reference. A statement negating The Principle presupposes a criterion of truth, and the criterion of truth in turn depends on correct self-reference. Such an attempt at falsification therefore constitutes no valid refutation; it exposes the instability of within-scale self-reference.

## 2. The Meta-Framework: The Triple \((S,R,C_S)\)

### 2.1 Basic Definition

Let \( X \) be the domain of discourse, and \( S \subseteq X \) the target system or the set of target objects. We introduce the triple:

\[
(S,R,C_S)
\]

in which:

- \( S \): the target system or set of target objects;
- \( R \): the generative rules, specifying which objects may be generated or proved by rule to belong to \( S \);
- \( C_S: X \to \{\text{should belong to }S,\ \text{should not belong to }S\} \): the criterion, specifying which objects ought, ontologically, to belong to \( S \).

The condition of completeness is:

\[
\forall x \in X,\;
\bigl(R(x) \land C_S(x)=\text{should belong to }S\bigr)
\;\Rightarrow\;
x \in S
\]

Put plainly: if every object that is generated by rule and that by the criterion ought to belong to \( S \) in fact falls inside \( S \), then \((S,R,C_S)\) is called complete.

### 2.2 \(C_S\) Is a Projection of The Principle

In the present version, \(C_S\) is not an external criterion independent of The Principle. It is the projection of The Principle into the domain \( S \):

\[
C_S(x)=\text{should belong to }S
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
x \text{ conforms to what The Principle stipulates in } S
\]

Therefore "ought to belong" is no longer vague. It is given by The Principle. The triple preserves logical clarity while restoring the governing power of The Principle.

### 2.3 Separating Generation from Judgment

The key improvement of the triple is the separation of \(R\) from \(C_S\):

- \(R\) answers "can it be generated or proved by rule";
- \(C_S\) answers "ought it to belong".

In a formal system, \(R\) is provability, and \(C_S\) is semantic truth or ontological oughtness. The two may coincide, or they may not. One of the core meanings of Gödel's theorems is precisely to expose this non-coincidence.

## 3. Five Kinds of Completeness

Under the framework \((S,R,C_S)\), "completeness" in different domains receives a unified characterization, while each has its own \(R\) and \(C_S\).

### 3.1 Everyday Completeness

Everyday completeness means that tools, institutions, works, and the like conform to their functional principle.

- \(S\): a class of everyday objects;
- \(R\): everyday fabrication or use rules;
- \(C_S\): what The Principle stipulates in that functional domain.

Example: a knife that can perform the cutting function is everyday-complete. If the knife is made according to the fabrication rules but cannot cut, it is incomplete.

### 3.2 Logical Completeness

Logical completeness usually means that the proof system of first-order logic is complete with respect to semantic consequence.

- \(S\): the set of first-order-logic valid formulas;
- \(R\): formal proof;
- \(C_S\): logical truth.

Gödel's completeness theorem (1929) shows that in first-order logic every valid formula is provable. That is:

\[
\Gamma \models \varphi \;\Rightarrow\; \Gamma \vdash \varphi
\]

This is the positive manifestation of The Principle at the logical level: \(R\) and \(C_S\) coincide.

### 3.3 Syntactic Completeness

Syntactic completeness means that for every sentence \(\varphi\), a theory \(T\) either proves \(\varphi\) or proves \(\neg \varphi\):

\[
\forall \varphi,\; T \vdash \varphi \;\lor\; T \vdash \neg \varphi
\]

- \(S\): the theorem set of the theory \(T\);
- \(R\): formal proof in \(T\);
- \(C_S\): truth in the domain of discourse that \(T\) characterizes.

Syntactic completeness is a closure requirement at the level of theory.

### 3.4 Model-Theoretic Completeness

Model-theoretic completeness means that all models of a theory \(T\) are elementarily equivalent. Equivalently, for every sentence \(\varphi\):

\[
T \models \varphi \quad \text{or} \quad T \models \neg \varphi
\]

- \(S\): the theory \(T\);
- \(R\): model-theoretic semantic consequence;
- \(C_S\): truth in all models of the theory.

Model-theoretic completeness and syntactic completeness essentially coincide in classical logic; they differ only in the angle of formulation: one starts from proof, the other from models.

### 3.5 Ontological Completeness

Ontological completeness means that a thing conforms to its own principle.

- \(S\): any thing or system;
- \(R\): the generative or unfolding rules of that thing;
- \(C_S\): what The Principle stipulates in that thing.

Ontological completeness is the most general notion of completeness in this paper, and it is the ground of the other notions of completeness.

### 3.6 The Five Kinds Compared

| Completeness type | \(S\) | \(R\) | \(C_S\) | Projection of The Principle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Everyday completeness | everyday objects | fabrication/use rules | functional oughtness | functional principle |
| Logical completeness | set of valid formulas | formal proof | logical truth | logical principle |
| Syntactic completeness | theorem set of the theory | theory proof | truth in the domain | principle of the theory |
| Model-theoretic completeness | theory | semantic consequence | truth in models | semantic principle |
| Ontological completeness | any thing | generative unfolding rules | its own principle | The Principle itself |

The five kinds share one closure paradigm, but they are not isomorphic in the mathematical sense. They are the fractal manifestations of one scale-free principle at different scales. The structures differ; the rule is one.

## 4. The Dependence of Mathematical-Logical Truth on The Principle

### 4.1 The Type of Dependence

"Mathematical-logical truth depends on The Principle" means at least the following:

1. The truthmaker of a logical truth is not a formal convention but the structure of the thing itself.
2. The objective meaning of the logical constants — negation, conjunction, implication, quantifiers — is in the end stipulated by The Principle.
3. Logical validity is not a pure game of symbols, but a mapping of ontological structure.
4. The laws of contradiction, identity, and excluded middle are not arbitrary rules of thought, but unfoldings of The Principle at the logical level.

This dependence is not causal dependence, not epistemological dependence, and not an empirical hypothesis. It is ontological participation: mathematical-logical truths participate in The Principle at their own level.

### 4.2 Logical Truth as the Formal Manifestation of Correct Self-Reference

The identity law \( A \to A \):

- The identity of a thing with its principle, abstracted to the propositional level;
- Correct self-reference in propositional form.

The law of contradiction \(\neg(A \land \neg A)\):

- What exists cannot be a thing and at the same time not a thing;
- Correct self-reference excludes the negated form of mutable self-reference.

The law of excluded middle \( A \lor \neg A \):

- Whether it holds universally depends on whether The Principle unfolds at the logical level into a bivalent structure;
- Intuitionistic logic may correspond to a different unfolding of The Principle.

### 4.3 The Limits of Formal Systems

Mathematical-logical truth depends on The Principle, but formal systems cannot fully express this dependence.

Formal systems are scaled:

- Object language and metalanguage are stratified;
- Syntax and semantics are stratified;
- Proof and truth are stratified.

The Principle is scale-free. A formal system that attempts scale-free self-reference within scale must necessarily slide into mutable self-reference. Gödel's theorems are exactly the proof of this slide.

## 5. The Position of Gödel's Theorems

### 5.1 Gödel's Completeness Theorem

Gödel's completeness theorem (1929) is a positive result about first-order logic:

> In first-order logic, every semantically valid formula is provable.

Within the framework of this paper:

- \(R\): formal proof;
- \(C_S\): logical truth;
- Conclusion: \(R\) covers \(C_S\); completeness holds at the logical level.

This is the positive manifestation of The Principle in the domain of logic.

### 5.2 Gödel's First Incompleteness Theorem

Gödel's first incompleteness theorem (1931) shows that:

> For any recursively axiomatized theory \(T\) that contains sufficient arithmetic and is consistent, there is a sentence \(\varphi\) such that \(T \nvdash \varphi\) and \(T \nvdash \neg \varphi\).

Within the framework of this paper:

- \(R\): formal proof in \(T\);
- \(C_S\): truth in the domain of discourse that \(T\) characterizes;
- Conclusion: \(R\) cannot cover \(C_S\); the theory is syntactically incomplete.

This is not a refutation of The Principle, but the proof that formal systems slide into mutable self-reference:

- The object referred to by the Gödel sentence \(G\) is "G is provable";
- "G is provable" in turn depends on the encoding of \(G\);
- The reference changes in the course of the process, so the system cannot stably decide \(G\).

### 5.3 Gödel's Second Incompleteness Theorem

Gödel's second incompleteness theorem shows that:

> Such a theory \(T\) cannot prove its own consistency.

Within the framework of this paper, this exposes the limits of self-reference and self-defense:

- A system cannot fully internalize its own criterion \(C_S\) as \(R\);
- Consistency, as the condition of a system being "should be correct", cannot be completely proved by the system itself;
- This is yet another typical instance of mutable self-reference.

### 5.4 The Correct Direction of Interpretation

Therefore, the correct relation is:

> The Principle → the completeness criterion \(C_S\) in each domain → Gödel's theorems as the proof that formal systems slide into mutable self-reference.

And not:

> Gödel's theorems → a proof or refutation of The Principle.

Gödel's theorems are themselves mathematical-logical truths, and hence they too depend on The Principle. They cannot be used to falsify The Principle, because an attempt at falsification itself slides into mutable self-reference.

## 6. Polysemy and Compatibility

### 6.1 Polysemy

Different domains all say "completeness", but they mean different things:

- Everyday completeness means conformity to function;
- Logical completeness means that a proof system covers logical truth;
- Syntactic completeness means that a theory decides every sentence;
- Model-theoretic completeness means elementary equivalence of models;
- Ontological completeness means that a thing conforms to its own principle.

To equate these "completenesses" directly is to commit polysemy. The value of the triple framework is precisely that it refines their differences into the differences between \(R\) and \(C_S\).

### 6.2 Structural Compatibility

Though they differ, they share one closure paradigm:

\[
\forall x \in X,\;
\bigl(R(x) \land C_S(x)=\text{should belong to }S\bigr)
\;\Rightarrow\;
x \in S
\]

Therefore they are not mutually irrelevant ambiguities, but projections of one and the same Principle into different domains. They are ontologically of one origin, formally not isomorphic.

### 6.3 Avoiding Two Errors

- The first error: mixing completions from different domains directly, producing polysemy;
- The second error: severing completions from different domains entirely, denying the compatibility among them.

The position of this paper is: distinguish without severing, unify without conflating.

## 7. Self-Reference, Circularity, and Infinite Regress

### 7.1 Correct Self-Reference Terminates Infinite Regress

The Principle, as correct self-reference, does not participate in infinite regress.

If The Principle itself were required to be judged by some external standard, two outcomes would follow:

- Either an infinite regress ensues: every standard requires a higher standard;
- Or a circularity ensues: in the end the standard supports only itself.

The Principle is correct self-reference: it refers to itself without changing itself. It is the terminus of all defense, but not a "first principle" in the hierarchical sense; it is the scale-free self-referential principle.

### 7.2 Mutable Self-Reference Produces Infinite Regress

Formal systems are scaled. When they attempt full self-reference, they necessarily slide into mutable self-reference:

- The object referred to keeps changing;
- The system cannot close;
- Every level requires a higher level to judge it, and that higher level in turn slides into mutable self-reference.

Gödel's theorems, the consistency sentence, and the truth predicate paradox are all typical instances of mutable self-reference.

### 7.3 Rejecting Falsificationism and Self-Reference

An attempt to falsify The Principle is itself mutable self-reference:

- A statement negating The Principle presupposes a criterion of truth;
- The criterion of truth in turn depends on correct self-reference;
- Therefore an attempt at falsification constitutes no valid refutation.

The Principle does not take part in the game of falsification. Unfalsifiability is not a defect, but a categorical feature of a scale-free self-referential principle.

## 8. Conclusion

Guided by the scale-free self-referential Principle of things, this paper proposes the triple \((S,R,C_S)\) as the meta-framework of completeness and reaches the following conclusions:

1. The ultimate meaning of completeness is that a thing conforms to its principle;
2. "Correctness is completeness" is a direct corollary of The Principle;
3. The completeness criteria \(C_S\) in different domains are domain projections of The Principle;
4. The five notions of completeness share the closure paradigm, but are not formally isomorphic;
5. Mathematical-logical truth depends on The Principle, and this dependence is ontological participation, not an empirical hypothesis;
6. Gödel's theorems are the proof that formal systems slide into mutable self-reference, not a falsification of The Principle;
7. Polysemy arises from conflating \(R\) with \(C_S\), and compatibility arises from their joint participation in The Principle;
8. The Principle, as correct self-reference, terminates infinite regress and the game of falsification;
9. Formal systems are scaled and cannot fully participate in the scale-free Principle of things.

Further work consists in making "correct", "relevant difference", and "objective relevance" concrete in each domain:

- **Correct**: conforming to The Principle;
- **Relevant difference**: the essential difference that bears on whether something belongs to \(S\), namely the difference stipulated by The Principle in \(S\);
- **Objective relevance**: relevance stipulated by The Principle, rather than relevance agreed upon by convention.

In this way "correctness is completeness" is no longer an empty postulate, but an unfolding of The Principle in concrete domains.

The Principle explicitly rejects falsificationism. It does not take part in the game of empirical science, and it does not accept the inference that "unfalsifiable = meaningless". It is the scale-free self-referential principle, and the condition that makes every discourse of completeness, every judgment of truth, and every game of falsification possible.
